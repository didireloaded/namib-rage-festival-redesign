# Reference 01 — The 20 AI Design Slop Indicators

Source: user-provided video titled **“20 AI Design Slop Indicators”**. The labels below follow the video. Their interpretation for websites is original, practical guidance. These are **questions to challenge generic defaults**, not a set of elements that can never be used.

## The complete 20-item catalogue

### 1. Tags & pills
**Symptom:** Almost every heading has an eyebrow badge, every feature is wrapped in a capsule, and each status has a colored chip.
**Why it fails:** Pills lose meaning when everything is a pill. It creates clutter and a generic SaaS aesthetic.
**Better approach:** Use simple text metadata, clear headlines and whitespace. Reserve chips for selectable filters or genuinely meaningful states.
**Good exception:** Search filters, “Sold out,” product size, ticket category, or a prominent compliance status.
**Audit:** If every card has a badge, remove most and see if hierarchy improves.

### 2. Excessive italics
**Symptom:** Large paragraphs, subtitles and multiple headlines are italicized for “editorial flair.”
**Why it fails:** Extended italic copy reduces scanning and feels mannered without narrative purpose.
**Better approach:** Keep functional text upright. Use an italic phrase intentionally within editorial or brand storytelling.
**Good exception:** A pull quote or approved brand typographic voice.

### 3. Instrument Serif
**Symptom:** An AI designer chooses Instrument Serif (or another popular editorial display serif) as an automatic shortcut to luxury.
**Why it fails:** Typographic personality becomes detached from the brand and looks interchangeable across sites.
**Better approach:** Choose fonts based on tone, language coverage, reading contexts, licensing, visual pairing and performance. Use established brand fonts when they exist.
**Good exception:** A genuine editorial identity where that exact font is deliberate.

### 4. Negative kerning
**Symptom:** Tight tracking across large headings causes characters to collide or become cramped, especially on narrow screens.
**Why it fails:** Sacrifices readability to chase a trendy screenshot.
**Better approach:** Adjust display spacing cautiously by size and font. Default to font-provided spacing for body and controls.
**Good exception:** A refined, tested display wordmark or carefully designed hero headline.

### 5. Over-the-top detailed UI
**Symptom:** A page shows too many controls, labels, metrics, tooltips and decoration at once.
**Why it fails:** Visual noise hides the primary action.
**Better approach:** Reveal complexity progressively. Show task-critical data immediately; place less-used detail in secondary screens or disclosure areas.
**Good exception:** Data-intensive dashboards built for experienced users with truly high information density.

### 6. Overusing dividers
**Symptom:** Horizontal rules separate nearly every paragraph, link, stat and card.
**Why it fails:** The site feels like a spreadsheet and loses rhythm.
**Better approach:** Establish sections with spacing, alignment, background changes and typography. Use borders where items must be explicitly separated.
**Good exception:** Dense tables, transaction records, menus, or lists needing clear row boundaries.

### 7. Color shades & opacities
**Symptom:** Dozens of barely distinguishable grays, tints and opacity values are sprinkled across elements.
**Why it fails:** Tokens lose semantic meaning and contrast becomes inconsistent.
**Better approach:** Use a compact palette with named roles: canvas, surface, text, muted text, border, action, success, caution, error.
**Good exception:** A well-specified editorial color system or accessible chart palette.

### 8. Containers with borders
**Symptom:** Every piece of content lives in a card with a border, shadow and rounded corner.
**Why it fails:** Nothing has compositional priority, and the page looks like stacked widgets.
**Better approach:** Start with an open grid and strong typographic grouping. Introduce containers where users need to select, compare, scan or act on a contained unit.
**Good exception:** Prices, product selections, filters, alert summaries, operational dashboard components.

### 9. Overly detailed diagrams
**Symptom:** Decorative arrows, boxes, connector lines and icons make a simple process seem technical.
**Why it fails:** Diagram complexity exceeds the concept it explains.
**Better approach:** Use a short numbered sequence, one illustration, or a concise visual model. Reserve diagrammatic fidelity for genuine complexity.
**Good exception:** Systems architecture, logistics, clinical or technical workflows where relationships matter.

### 10. Verbose, redundant text
**Symptom:** Sections have an eyebrow, a title, a summary, a second restatement and a paragraph repeating the same claim.
**Why it fails:** Increases scroll without communicating new information.
**Better approach:** Write one strong idea per section; use proof, examples and clear calls to action. Cut filler and generic slogans.
**Good exception:** Legal disclosures, product limitations, educational documentation and safety-critical instructions.

### 11. Code breadcrumbs
**Symptom:** Fake paths, pseudo-terminal commands, technical file labels and code snippets decorate a consumer marketing page.
**Why it fails:** Internal developer metaphors distract from the user story.
**Better approach:** Use actual product imagery and ordinary language. Show code only when developers are the audience or the code is real and useful.
**Good exception:** Developer documentation, API marketing, code editors and technical tools.

### 12. Section numbering
**Symptom:** Every section is labelled “01 / 02 / 03” solely to look crafted.
**Why it fails:** Artificial sequential framing implies a process where none exists.
**Better approach:** Use descriptive headings and clear section boundaries.
**Good exception:** Actual step-by-step procedures, ordered case studies, chronological storytelling.

### 13. Eyebrows
**Symptom:** Tiny uppercase text appears over virtually every heading, often repeating the heading.
**Why it fails:** Creates a monotonous rhythm and too much small text.
**Better approach:** Use contextual kickers only when they add categorisation or meaning.
**Good exception:** Clearly distinct editorial categories, event series or navigation sections.

### 14. Dithering
**Symptom:** Grain, noisy halftones and pixel textures are added indiscriminately to make layouts feel “designed.”
**Why it fails:** Interferes with readability, clarity and image quality.
**Better approach:** Favor clean rendering and authentic material. If texture is essential, keep it controlled and away from small text.
**Good exception:** Retro visual identities or creative portfolios with approved art direction.

### 15. ASCII images
**Symptom:** Decorative character art stands in for meaningful photography, illustration or icons.
**Why it fails:** Weak semantics and potential accessibility issues.
**Better approach:** Use real imagery, semantic diagrams or genuine vector illustrations where they help.
**Good exception:** Terminal tools, code aesthetics, textual artworks and retro brands.

### 16. Squiggles
**Symptom:** Random doodles, curly arrows, brush lines and underlines decorate most headings.
**Why it fails:** Emphasis becomes noisy and is rarely tied to brand identity.
**Better approach:** Use typographic contrast, composition, image cropping and color for emphasis.
**Good exception:** Youthful campaigns, hand-drawn visual systems, genuine illustrator-created assets.

### 17. Excessive mono fonts
**Symptom:** Body text, labels, menus and marketing copy all use monospace as an all-purpose modern-tech style.
**Why it fails:** Long-form reading becomes uncomfortable and brand tone is flattened.
**Better approach:** Use a legible proportional face for UI/body copy; mono for code, times, IDs or technical data.
**Good exception:** A deliberate developer or experimental editorial site, tested for reading comfort.

### 18. ALL CAPS subtitles
**Symptom:** Nearly every caption, subsection and supporting line is uppercase, letter-spaced and tiny.
**Why it fails:** Looks templated and slows reading.
**Better approach:** Use sentence case and distinguish content with size, weight and location. Reserve caps for short, conventional labels.
**Good exception:** Navigation micro-labels or formal brand systems.

### 19. Gradient backgrounds
**Symptom:** The website defaults to a glowing purple/blue gradient, giant abstract blur blobs and gradient buttons.
**Why it fails:** Often hides the absence of a strong visual idea and resembles countless generic AI pages.
**Better approach:** Start with content, imagery, type and grid. Use a solid surface or real image unless a gradient belongs to the identity.
**Good exception:** An established brand gradient, visualized data field, event atmosphere or carefully tuned art direction.

### 20. Periods in titles
**Symptom:** Every short headline ends with a full stop for forced editorial seriousness.
**Why it fails:** It becomes an overused visual tic instead of natural language.
**Better approach:** Follow brand voice and normal punctuation. Short navigational headings often need none.
**Good exception:** Full sentences, editorial titles where punctuation is intentional.

## Common compound failure patterns

**The generic SaaS landing page:** oversized centered heading, three chips, gradient behind a UI mockup, six equal icon cards, four opaque purple metrics, a feature table of invented claims, and a black CTA strip. Repair by grounding the story in the actual product, real screenshots and customer jobs. Vary section types and show genuine evidence.

**The fake luxury website:** serif italics everywhere, tiny spaced-out caps, giant whitespace with no story, abstract stock photography, too much opacity and gold gradients. Repair with strong type choices, authentic materials and a purposeful photographic treatment.

**The over-carded services site:** one rounded border box per service, testimonial, contact method, process step and FAQ. Repair with a strong visual hierarchy, honest examples, service groupings and functional enquiry paths.

**The chaotic creative portfolio:** autoplay everything, unbounded transitions, illegible overlays, inconsistent navigation and hidden projects. Repair with curated visual narrative, controls, captions, a stable browsing structure, media optimization and reduced-motion support.

**The crowded dashboard:** labels below 12px, every item in a card, alert badges everywhere and no prioritization. Repair with proper information density, clear status hierarchy, navigable tables and one obvious work queue.

## Twenty-point audit method

For each indicator mark **Present**, **Justified**, **Unjustified**, or **Not applicable**. Explain the context and the replacement. Do not mechanically remove every matching element.

Critical questions:

1. Would this page still communicate its main proposition without decorative pills, tiny eyebrows or gradients?
2. Does every distinct section offer new information, proof or action?
3. Is the chosen typography actually right for the brand and for long-form reading?
4. Does the hierarchy still work without unnecessary boxes and lines?
5. Is the page visually specific to its audience rather than an imitation of a popular AI template?
6. Is the alternative as clear or clearer for a keyboard, touch and screen reader user?
